Optimizing complex systems on exascale and GPU platforms

GPUs, AI accelerators and heterogeneous multi-node supercomputers are an opportunity to tackle problems that could not be conceived before, but they require re-designing the classical algorithms of discrete optimization, since branch-and-bound, local search and decomposition were all engineered for CPUs. I work on both ends of that: decomposition that runs across the partitions of a supercomputer, and search that is native to the GPU rather than a port of a CPU method. My training here includes the Argonne Training Program on Extreme-Scale Computing.

Results so far

  • Facility location under congestion at metropolitan scale. Planning where to open facilities and how much capacity to install, for the hundreds of thousands of customers of a metropolitan area and with queueing at the facilities, yields a model with billions of variables and constraints that cannot even be loaded in the memory of a solver. For the charging of electric delivery fleets, a Lagrangian dual decomposition that splits the model by station exploits parallelism across 680 CPU cores on heterogeneous partitions that cannot communicate with one another, solving thousands of subproblems per iteration by cutting planes and recovering feasible plans by a three-step rounding heuristic. It solved an instance of the Chicago metropolitan area with nearly half a million households and two thousand candidate stations, a size never addressed in the literature. The scheduling of subproblems across partitions that cannot communicate, overlooked by existing parallel decomposition methods, is ongoing work.
  • Primal heuristics native to the GPU. Attempts to run the primal heuristics of mixed-integer programming on GPUs have so far been rather disappointing. With a collaborator at the University of Florida, I replace the repeated linear relaxations of classical diving with a simultaneous enumeration of all 2k combinations of fixing k integer variables, one per GPU thread with domain propagation between fixings. The dives are independent and follow the same instruction path, so there is no warp divergence, and the method has no meaningful CPU counterpart: the enumeration it relies on is viable only on a GPU, and the depth grows with GPU memory. A first implementation finds feasible solutions on as many competition instances as an open-source solver within five minutes, and better solutions than the solver on a quarter of them.
  • Railcar blocking by GPU-accelerated neighborhood search. Railroads sort cars into blocks that travel together between classification yards, and changing a single block reroutes cars across the network, so evaluating candidate moves dominates local search over blocking plans. In ongoing work, a very large-scale neighborhood search computes the cost of all candidate moves exactly on the GPU from a factorization of the plan’s savings, and prices more than half a million candidate moves in less time than the conventional evaluation of a single move.

Where this is going

First, a GPU-native solver for vehicle routing, released as open-source software portable across GPU architectures and building on my contributions to NVIDIA’s cuOpt and to VRPSolverEasy. In it, local and large neighborhood search run as divergence-free kernels, and a first-order method solves the linear relaxation in mixed precision to bound their solutions, so that the routing and clearing problems of my first two thrusts become re-solvable in real time on a single GPU, for fleets that run without drivers. Second, the freight infrastructure of a nation, from terminals and truck parking to the charging and refueling of fleets, planned in one model on the DOE’s leadership-class supercomputers, with demand modeled at the level of households and fleets at the level of trucks; this needs provably convergent decomposition across millions of subproblems and the partitions of an exascale machine. Third, the neural surrogates and value functions of my first two thrusts are trained on GPUs and AI accelerators while the mixed-integer programs that embed them are solved on CPUs; optimizing over learned models and integer decisions end to end on a single device is an open problem.
